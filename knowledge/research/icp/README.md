## Research purpose

Understand the career challenges experienced by Product Designers and Design Systems Designers, the solutions they currently use, and what they value enough to pay for.

Use this research to inform:

- ICP selection
- Positioning
- Offer development
- Content strategy
- Customer discovery
- Sales messaging

## Interview coverage

Nine designers: Edison, Anna, Erika, Jesus, Josh, Malu, Kalyani, Weena, Mike.

## Research principles

1. Distinguish reported facts from interpretation.
2. Do not assume portfolio problems cause interview rejections.
3. Do not treat hypothetical willingness to pay as a confirmed purchase.
4. Do not treat all designers as one customer segment.
5. Preserve negative cases and conflicting evidence.
6. Use interview findings to generate hypotheses, not to claim product-market fit.
7. Do not invent quotes, statistics or interview details.
8. Real names may live in `knowledge/`; never publish them in outbound content.

## Emerging patterns

### Pattern 1: Uncertainty about what to improve

Observed across: Edison, Erika, Jesus, Weena, Mike.

Designers are unsure which projects, experiences and capabilities to emphasise. They may repeatedly revise their portfolios without knowing whether the changes are valuable.

**Hypothesis:** Designers may value help identifying and prioritising the changes most relevant to their target roles.

**Not yet proven:** That this problem is urgent enough for most designers to purchase Storyline's intensive.

### Pattern 2: Generic feedback is insufficient

Observed across: Anna, Weena, Mike.

Designers report limitations with generic mentoring, AI feedback or peer reviews.

**Hypothesis:** Specialist, evidence-based feedback may be more compelling than general portfolio advice.

**Not yet proven:** That Storyline's methodology is sufficiently differentiated to influence purchasing decisions.

### Pattern 3: Difficulty demonstrating relevant experience

Observed across: Edison, Anna, Erika, Jesus, Weena, Mike.

Challenges include communicating seniority, demonstrating strategic impact, positioning transferable skills, selecting case studies, demonstrating specialist capabilities, and matching experience to target roles.

**Hypothesis:** Role-specific evidence mapping may address a recurring challenge among experienced designers.

**Not yet proven:** That customers understand or value evidence mapping before experiencing it.

### Pattern 4: Uncertainty creates unnecessary work

Observed across: Edison, Jesus, Josh, Weena, Mike.

Designers may spend considerable time revising portfolios, creating additional projects or comparing alternatives.

**Hypothesis:** Clarity and prioritisation may be valuable outcomes independent of portfolio execution.

### Pattern 5: Existing paid alternatives

Confirmed spending:

- Edison: specialist mentoring
- Anna: mentoring and education
- Weena: general career coaching
- Malu: career coaching
- Kalyani: career training

Considered spending:

- Jesus: hypothetical interest around $500
- Mike: actively researching mentoring; stated comfort up to $300

**Interpretation:** Some designers spend money on career development and professional feedback.

**Limitation:** This does not prove demand for Storyline's specific services or prices.

## Potential customer segments

These segments are provisional groupings from the dataset — not a locked ICP.

### Segment A: Experienced designers struggling to demonstrate seniority

Examples: Edison, Erika

Core challenge: communicating impact, contribution and strategic capability.

Potential Storyline fit: role-specific evidence selection and case study positioning.

### Segment B: Designers transitioning roles or industries

Examples: Weena, Jesus

Core challenge: making existing experience relevant to unfamiliar roles or industries.

Potential Storyline fit: transferable-skills positioning and evidence mapping.

### Segment C: Designers seeking expert portfolio diagnosis

Examples: Mike, Anna

Core challenge: existing feedback is insufficient, and they don't know what to improve.

Potential Storyline fit: focused expert portfolio review or strategy audit.

### Segment D: Designers needing broader career direction

Examples: Josh, Malu

Core challenge: networking, visibility, prioritisation or broader job-search strategy.

Potential Storyline fit: uncertain or weaker fit for a portfolio-specific offer.

### Segment E: Junior designers and career switchers

Example: Kalyani

Core challenge: limited experience, demonstrating capabilities and accessing opportunities.

Potential Storyline fit: possible future educational audience, but outside the current primary ICP.

## Competitive alternatives

- General career coaches
- Specialist design mentors
- Portfolio review sessions
- Career programmes and boot camps
- AI tools such as Claude
- Free advice and online resources
- Peer feedback
- Portfolio inspiration websites
- Independent case study revisions
- Doing nothing or delaying improvements

### Positioning research questions

- Why do designers choose one alternative over another?
- What triggers the decision to seek paid help?
- What outcomes do they expect?
- What disappoints them about existing options?
- What makes an expert credible?
- Do they prefer diagnosis, education or collaborative execution?
- How do they assess whether a service is worth the price?

## Storyline positioning hypotheses

These are hypotheses, not validated conclusions.

**A.** Experienced designers value knowing what needs improving more than receiving another portfolio framework.

**B.** A focused expert portfolio diagnosis may be a more accessible entry point than a comprehensive workshop.

**C.** Designers transitioning industries or specialisations may particularly value help communicating transferable experience.

**D.** Research-informed feedback may differentiate Storyline from generic mentoring.

**E.** The underlying Storyline methodology is: Target role → Hiring signals → Relevant evidence → Case study decisions. This should be tested against customer needs rather than assumed to be the primary purchasing motivation.
